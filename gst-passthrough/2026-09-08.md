Singapore raised the goods and services tax from 7 to 8 per cent on 1 January 2023,
and from 8 to 9 per cent on 1 January 2024. Ask anyone what that did to prices and
you will get a confident answer. Things went up. Hawkers used it as cover. The
official line agrees on direction, if not on blame: both the Monetary Authority of
Singapore and the Ministry of Trade and Industry put the contribution of each
increase at about one percentage point of inflation.

I went looking for that one percentage point in the national price index. It is not
there. Not because the tax failed to reach prices, but because the index cannot see
something that small. What I found instead was that the tax is perfectly visible in a
handful of prices, and one of them is on your water bill.

## Everyone knows the GST rise made things dearer

The belief is reasonable and held by serious people. Inflation was running at 6.6 per
cent year on year in January 2023, the month the first increase landed, and people
complained: the government's Committee Against Profiteering took 286 pieces of
feedback between April 2022 and January 2023, of which 26 alleged that a business had
misrepresented the GST increase.

So the question is not whether prices rose. It is how much of the rise was the tax.

## The tax was smaller than one month of noise

Start with what full pass-through actually means, because it is not one per cent.

A rise from 7 to 8 per cent does not add a percentage point to prices. It multiplies
the tax-inclusive price by 1.08 divided by 1.07, which is a rise of 0.9346 per cent.
The second increase, from 8 to 9, multiplies by 1.09 over 1.08, or 0.9259 per cent.
On a five dollar meal, the first increase is 4.7 cents and both together are 9.3
cents.

Now put that next to how much the index moves anyway. Between 2010 and 2026, the
month-on-month change in the All Items index going into January ranged from minus
0.75 per cent to plus 1.63 per cent, with a standard deviation of 0.61 per cent
across the years without a tax change.

The thing being measured is smaller than the ordinary variation of the thing
measuring it.

## So why can nobody find it in the index?

Because in a fight between a 0.93 per cent signal and 0.61 per cent of noise, one
observation is not enough.

I estimated the January 2023 step against what the same index did in every other
January since 2010, controlling for Chinese New Year. The answer is an excess of
minus 0.02 per cent, with a 95 per cent interval from minus 1.42 to plus 1.39. That
contains full pass-through. It also contains zero, and everything in between.

This is not a result. It is the absence of one, and the absence is the point. A
single event of this size gives a test with 35 per cent power; reaching the
conventional 80 would need four rate changes, and Singapore has had two in the period
with usable data. I committed in advance to publishing no number if the January
placebo tests threw up movements as large as the tax. They did.

There is also a reason to think January was never where the money was. MAS's own
October 2023 review says the 2023 increase "was staggered across several months,
instead of passing through fully at the start of the year", and that major
supermarket chains announced temporary absorption or token discounts. If that is
right, a January-only test is looking in the wrong month.

So I ran the staggered version too. It does not help: cumulating months adds noise
faster than it adds signal, and the aggregate cannot see a spread-out tax rise either.

{{< aside title="What the pre-registered tests actually did" >}}
Six tests were written down before the data was downloaded, each with a stated fail
condition, and the file was not edited afterwards.

The headline design was a difference-in-differences using GST-exempt categories as a
control group. It failed its parallel trends test, with a pre-trend of plus 0.159 log
points per month and p below 0.0001. That failure was predicted in advance and for
the stated reason: the only exempt category with real weight in the index is
accommodation, and Singapore rents were in an extraordinary cycle through the
pre-period. A control group riding a rental boom is not a control group.

Two further things had to be corrected during the analysis. Standard errors on a
single treated observation are degenerate, because the treatment dummy fits that
point exactly and leaves no residual behind, so all inference here uses a prediction
interval instead. And Chinese New Year fell on 22 January in 2023, the earliest date
in the whole window, against 10 February in 2024. That pushes the two estimates in
opposite directions, which is exactly the raw pattern before the control was added.
MTI named the same confound in its own January 2023 release.

On the staggered version: cumulating the change from December through each month to
June, the 2023 estimates do drift upward, reaching 0.86 per cent by April against a
target of 0.93, which is the shape MAS describes. But the interval widens faster than
the estimate grows. By April the same calculation applied to years with no tax change
ranges from minus 1.9 to plus 2.0 per cent, and 2023 ranks twelfth of sixteen. The
2024 path does not drift upward at all, which is consistent with MAS expecting no
absorption that year but is equally consistent with the basket revision sitting in
that month.
{{< /aside >}}

## January is the worst possible month to look

Four of Singapore's five GST increases took effect on 1 January. That is
administratively sensible and analytically terrible.

January is when transport fares change, when school fees reset, when insurance
renews, and when Chinese New Year arrives in some years but not others. None of that
is the tax. The fifth increase, from 5 to 7 per cent in July 2007, avoids all of it,
and does produce a step of 1.82 per cent against a mechanical figure of 1.89.

That looks like the clean answer until you take it apart. Housing and utilities
contribute 63 per cent of it, and Singapore property was in a boom in mid-2007. The
one increase big enough to clear the noise lands on top of something else large.

## Water is the one price that holds still

Most prices move in January for reasons unrelated to tax. Some do not, and that is
where the question can be answered.

Water's price index moved six times in eleven years, and every move is a dated
administrative decision.

| When | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| July 2017 | +15.10% | PUB price revision, first phase |
| July 2018 | +14.70% | PUB price revision, second phase |
| January 2023 | +1.02% | GST, 7 to 8 per cent |
| January 2024 | +0.97% | GST, 8 to 9 per cent |
| April 2024 | +6.99% | PUB, plus 20 cents per cubic metre |
| April 2025 | +10.46% | PUB, plus 30 cents per cubic metre |

PUB's release of 27 September 2023 confirms that record: the previous revision was
2017, the next two April 2024 and April 2025. No water price changed in any January. It also notes that all its figures are
before GST, which means the consumer pays the tariff multiplied by one plus the rate.

So the two January moves are the tax and nothing else. They are plus 1.02 and plus
0.97 per cent, against statutory amounts of 0.93 and 0.93. In the nine other
Januaries the price did not move at all.

That is also why water answers a question the shops cannot. A supermarket can absorb
a tax rise for three months as a promotion, and MAS says several did. PUB cannot.
Where a price is set by arithmetic the tax lands in full and at once. Where someone
decides what the label should say, it arrives late, partially, or rounded.

{{< figure src="fig2-water-supply.svg" caption="Figure 2. Nine Januaries at exactly zero, and two at the size of the tax. Month-on-month change in the water supply price index into each January, 2016 to 2026, in per cent. The line marks full statutory pass-through of 0.93 per cent." >}}

Water is not alone, just cleanest. Thirty-two of the 162 index series move so little
in an ordinary January that a one per cent step would show. Across those 32, the
median excess January move averages minus 0.01 in every year without a tax change. In
January 2023 it is plus 0.68 and in January 2024 plus 0.45: 8.2 and 5.5 standard
deviations above the rest, and the two largest values in seventeen years.

{{< figure src="fig3-still-prices.svg" caption="Figure 3. In the prices that otherwise hold still, the two tax years are the only two that move. Median excess January change in log points across the 32 lowest-variance series, 2010 to 2026." >}}

The mirror holds in one of the two years. The Ministry of Finance says the government
absorbs GST on publicly subsidised education, so those fees should not have moved. In
January 2023 polytechnic fees moved exactly zero. In January 2024 they moved 0.14 per
cent, a seventh of the tax but not nothing. That is the only January this series has
moved in eleven years, and it is also the month SingStat changed the basket. I cannot
separate those two, so the prediction is confirmed once and not twice.

## What would have to be true for me to be wrong

Three things would break this.

If PUB had quietly adjusted water tariffs in either January, the cleanest case
collapses. Its own release puts the neighbouring revisions in 2017 and April 2024, so
I do not think it did, but a tariff schedule showing otherwise would end the argument.

If the low-variance selection were manufacturing the result, running it on a year with
no tax change would also throw up a cluster of one per cent moves. I ran it on every
January from 2010 to 2026. It gives approximately zero every time except 2023 and 2024.

And this part was not pre-registered. It came out of the analysis rather than being
planned before it, which makes it a hypothesis for someone else to test rather than a
settled finding.

## The official one point is not a measurement

MAS forecast 2023 core inflation at 3.5 to 4.5 per cent including the GST increase
and 2.5 to 3.5 per cent excluding it. MTI forecast 2024 headline and core at 2.5 to
3.5 per cent including and 1.5 to 2.5 per cent excluding. Both differences are
exactly one percentage point, for two increases whose mechanical effects differ,
0.9346 against 0.9259.

They are equal because the figure is not a measurement, and MAS says so itself. Its
October 2023 review states that for the second increase, "the upcoming GST hike is
assumed to pass through fully in January under the current baseline". Its April 2023
review footnotes the first one the same way, as an expectation that full pass-through
"will occur over the course of 2023", and calls it "subject to significant
uncertainty".

An assumption of full pass-through is a sensible thing to put in a forecast. It is
not evidence about what firms did, and it should not be repeated as though it were.
For headline inflation it is also too big on its own terms: roughly a fifth of that
basket carries no GST at all, so full pass-through across the whole index works out
at 0.66 to 0.75 points, not 1.0.

MAS was careful here in a way the figure's later users have not been. The same April
review notes that pass-through in January 2023 was "relatively modest compared to
that seen during the previous GST increase in 2007".

## The 80 per cent I cannot see

This does not tell you what happened to your hawker bill. The method works precisely
where prices are boring, and says nothing about the four fifths of the basket that
moves in January for its own reasons, which are the prices people complain about. If
restaurants over-shifted, this cannot show it. If they under-shifted, it cannot show
that either.

Nor can it settle January 2024 in the aggregate. SingStat rebased the index to 2024
weights, and the new basket begins in January 2024, the same month as the second
increase. Recomputing that month's year-on-year rate on today's series gives 3.11 per
cent where 2.9 was published at the time: the revision alone moved the number by 0.21
points, a fifth of the entire official GST effect. The series that would settle it,
the 2019-base index published throughout 2024, has been withdrawn from data.gov.sg,
where the dataset page now returns "dataset not found". Water escapes this, because
one item's index does not depend on how items are weighted together.

What the data supports is narrow. In prices that change for no other reason, both
increases arrived within the month and at roughly the full statutory amount.
Everywhere else the national index is too blunt an instrument to tell you, and the
official one percentage point is not the measurement people take it for.
